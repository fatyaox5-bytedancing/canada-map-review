## 二、旁白正文（英文）

### 第 1 章｜*A Country That Looks Empty*（141 词）

**1.1**（20 词）

> This is Canada. Ten million square kilometres of land, and from up here, almost none of it looks lived in.

**1.2**（75 词）

> Officially, a population centre needs at least a thousand people, and a density of four hundred people per square kilometre. Now watch the six largest population centres in the country light up. A population centre is where people are concentrated — as Statistics Canada counts them. Between any two of these six there are fifteen distances, and the median of those fifteen is about two thousand seven hundred kilometres. Not the closest pair. The middle one.

**1.3**（22 词）

> The closest of those six are still a hundred and fifty-seven kilometres apart. The farthest are more than three thousand kilometres apart.

**1.4**（24 词）

> The six largest are spread across thousands of kilometres, from Vancouver on the Pacific coast to Montreal in the east. So where is everybody?

---

### 第 2 章｜*Two Squares*（216 词）

**2.1**（49 词）

> Here is the answer, and it is stranger than the question. Lay two squares, two hundred kilometres on a side, over this country. One over Toronto. One over Montreal. Together they cover about eighty thousand square kilometres. Inside them live forty-three percent of everyone in a Canadian population centre.

**2.2**（64 词）

> And the two squares are not even copies of each other. The Toronto square contains eighty-nine population centres and eight and a half million people. The Montreal one contains ninety-six centres and four and a half million. Same area, but inside the Toronto square a centre averages about ninety-five thousand people, and inside the Montreal square about forty-eight thousand. More centres, half the people.

**2.3**（57 词）

> The provinces agree. Ontario holds forty-one percent of that total, Quebec twenty-three, British Columbia fourteen. Those shares are of the combined population of the one thousand and twenty-six population centres, not of Canada. Those are the three largest; the other ten provinces and territories together hold about twenty-two percent. And the three provinces together hold seventy-eight percent.

**2.4**（47 词）

> So the population is not spread across the country. It is stacked, very high, in a very small number of places. Across all one thousand and twenty-six population centres, the median population is about three thousand. The average is nearly thirty thousand, close to ten times larger.

---

### 第 3 章｜*One Corridor, Two Different Things*（413 词）

**3.1**（70 词）

> Those places run in a corridor: population centres strung across southern Ontario and Quebec, following the St. Lawrence. Inside the map we drew for that corridor, four hundred and fourteen population centres hold about fifty-nine percent. That is a share of the people in Canada's population centres — not of the country's population, and not of the number of centres. But three of the six largest are far to the west.

**3.2**（33 词）

> The corridor is not one uniform band. It has three peaks: Toronto, Ottawa-Gatineau, and Montreal. Between Toronto and Ottawa, the band thins out, and that stretch is the weakest point anywhere along it.

**3.3**（119 词）

> Slice the corridor into bands half a degree of longitude wide, and the sizes change sharply. West of Toronto, four of those bands together hold a hundred and nine population centres; Toronto's own band holds thirty-one. Those four are all the corridor contains west of Toronto. Yet Toronto's band has more than twice the people. Its centres average about two hundred thousand people each; in the four bands to the west, about twenty-seven thousand. That average is dominated by Toronto itself, which accounts for about five and a half million of the six and a quarter million people in this band. The median tells a less dramatic story: the typical population centre is much smaller than the average suggests.

**3.4**（48 词）

> Now measure it properly. Sample points along the line from Toronto to Ottawa, every five kilometres. Just over twenty-one percent of those points have no population centre within twenty-five kilometres. The largest single gap is fifty-two kilometres wide. Note the denominator: this percentage counts sample points, not people.

**3.5**（136 词）

> Then the line turns to Montreal, and the picture changes. Sampling the same way from Ottawa to Montreal, across a hundred and fifty-seven kilometres, none of the thirty-two sample points has its nearest population centre beyond twenty-five kilometres. And from Montreal to Quebec City, over two hundred and thirty-two kilometres, again none of the forty-seven. Along those two stretches, every sample point stays within twenty-five kilometres of some population centre. Zero percent does not mean the land is empty. It means the spacing here stays under twenty-five kilometres the whole way. That measures the spacing of these centres. It does not mean the land between them is empty. It is also a measurement, not a physical fact: it uses five-kilometre sampling and a twenty-five kilometre threshold. Measure with a tighter threshold and more gaps would appear.

**3.6**（17 词）

> Two neighbouring segments of the same corridor. Twenty-one percent, and zero percent. Nobody has yet explained why.

---

### 第 4 章｜*The Other Half*（221 词）

**4.1**（101 词）

> Now fly north of the sixtieth parallel. Two different ways of defining the north give two different counts. The three northern territories hold twelve population centres and sixty-nine thousand people. North of the line, there are fourteen, with seventy-one thousand five hundred and ninety-two people — about zero point two four percent of everyone living in a population centre in Canada. Do not add those two counts together. The twelve territory centres are already inside the fourteen; the two are different ways of drawing one line. And that line is only a reference; the north is not actually divided at sixty degrees.

**4.2**（58 词）

> These are real places. Whitehorse, Yellowknife, Iqaluit. Fourteen of them, with stores and schools and airports, spread across Canada's far north. The nearest neighbour of Kuujjuaq is five hundred and fifty kilometres away. Inuvik's is five hundred and forty. Whitehorse's is four hundred and thirty. Up here, the nearest town is often in another territory, or another province.

**4.3**（63 词）

> What we can say is where these places are, and how many people live in them. What we cannot say is why the pattern looks this way. Answering that would take data this film does not have: climate, terrain, which land can actually be built on, and a century of change in each of these places. And so it stays an open question.

---

### 第 5 章｜*What This Map Does and Doesn't Say*（196 词）

**5.1**（56 词）

> So here is everything we measured. Two squares: forty-three percent. Ontario and Quebec: sixty-three percent. One corridor in the east: about fifty-nine percent of the people in Canada's population centres. Those percentages share one denominator: the people who live in population centres, not Canada's population. Three different measurements, three different scales, all pointing the same way.

**5.2**（51 词）

> And the opposite is also true. Take the thirty-four largest population centres, spread right across the country. Pair them up every possible way — five hundred and sixty-one pairs — and the median distance across all of them is still about one thousand four hundred kilometres. Not the nearest pairs. The middle one.

**5.3**（54 词）

> One last thing, and it matters. Thirty million, three hundred and eighty-nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine. That is the combined population of one thousand and twenty-six population centres. It is not the population of Canada. About six and a half million people live outside these boundaries, and this map does not include them.

**5.4**（34 词）

> So this map tells us where people are. It does not tell us why they are there. Measuring the pattern is the easy part. Explaining it is the problem we are still stuck on.
